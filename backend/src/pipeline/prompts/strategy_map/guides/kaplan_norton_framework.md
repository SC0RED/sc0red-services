# Kaplan & Norton Strategy Map Framework

This guide distils the canonical Balanced Scorecard / Strategy Map
framework introduced by Robert S. Kaplan and David P. Norton (HBR,
September–October 2000, "Having Trouble with Your Strategy? Then Map
It"). Use it as the THEORETICAL FOUNDATION for every strategy-map
generation. The Vector Advisory house style (encoded separately in
`vector_style_guide.md`) builds on top of this framework but does not
replace it.

## The four perspectives, in causal order

A strategy map specifies cause-and-effect relationships across four
perspectives. Read bottom-up: investments in capacity drive process
improvements, which deliver customer outcomes, which produce
financial results.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   FINANCIAL                — outcomes (revenue, profit, cost)    │  ← Lagging
│        ▲                                                          │
│   CUSTOMER                 — what customers experience            │
│        ▲                                                          │
│   INTERNAL PROCESSES       — what we do operationally             │
│        ▲                                                          │
│   ORGANIZATIONAL CAPACITY  — people, technology, culture          │  ← Leading
│   (Kaplan & Norton call                                           │
│   this "Learning and                                              │
│   Growth"; we call it                                             │
│   "Organizational Capacity")                                      │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The arrows are the load-bearing element. K&N: "A strategy map
specifies these cause-and-effect relationships, which makes them
explicit and TESTABLE. The key, then, to implementing strategy is to
have everyone in the organization clearly understand the underlying
hypotheses, to test the hypotheses continually, and to use those
results to adapt as required."

A strategy map without arrows is a list of objectives, not a strategy.

## The three customer value propositions (Treacy & Wiersema, 1995)

The Customer perspective is anchored to ONE of three generic value
propositions. Companies excel at ONE while maintaining threshold
performance in the other two.

| Proposition           | What customers care about                           | Brand exemplars        |
|----------------------|------------------------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Operational Excellence | Competitive pricing, product quality and selection, | McDonald's, Dell       |
|                       | speedy order fulfilment, on-time delivery           |                        |
| Customer Intimacy     | Exceptional service, completeness of solutions,     | Home Depot, IBM        |
|                       | long-term relationships, knowing the customer       | (in the 1960s–70s)     |
| Product Leadership    | Functionality, features, overall product            | Intel, Sony            |
|                       | performance, being best-in-class                    |                        |

K&N caveat: "approximately three-quarters of executive teams do not
have consensus about this basic information." Even at the leadership
level, value-proposition classification is hard. When generating a
strategy map from public data, this means:

- Always justify the classification with a one-sentence rationale.
- If the company shows two strong propositions (the Mobil case in the
  HBR article shows customer intimacy + operational excellence), use
  the `hybrid` classification and name the secondary explicitly.
- Frame the classification as a HYPOTHESIS for the deep-dive
  conversation, not as an authoritative diagnosis.

## The four internal-process categories

Every strategy map's Internal Processes perspective draws from four
high-level process categories. Coverage across all four indicates a
balanced strategy; gaps may be deliberate but more often signal
strategic blind spots.

1. **Build the franchise** — innovation processes. New products and
   services, new markets and customer segments, R&D pipeline.
2. **Increase customer value** — customer-management processes.
   Deepen relationships, expand share of wallet, retain and grow
   existing customers, customer-experience design.
3. **Achieve operational excellence** — operations and logistics
   processes. Supply chain, manufacturing, distribution, cost,
   quality, cycle time, capacity utilisation, asset management.
4. **Be a good corporate citizen** — regulatory, environmental,
   community, and governance processes. Compliance, sustainability,
   community presence.

K&N warning: "while many companies espouse a strategy that calls for
innovation or for developing value-adding customer relationships,
they mistakenly choose to measure only the cost and quality of their
operations — and not their innovations or their customer management
processes. These companies have a complete disconnect between their
strategy and how they measure it."

## Organizational Capacity triad (Vector renames "Learning & Growth")

The foundation perspective defines the core competencies, technologies,
and corporate culture that enable everything above. Three buckets:

- **People** — employee competencies, skills, talent development,
  leadership, succession.
- **Technology** — information systems, infrastructure, data, tools,
  technical capability.
- **Culture** — values, ways of working, behavioural norms,
  organisational alignment.

(Note: the Vector Advisory house style renames this perspective from
"Learning and Growth" to "Organizational Capacity". The substance is
the same; the rename is positioning. See `vector_style_guide.md`.)

The financial benefits from improved capacity reveal themselves in
stages:

- **Operational improvements** → short-term cash savings.
- **Customer-relationship improvements** → intermediate-term revenue
  growth.
- **Innovation improvements** → long-term revenue and margin growth.

## Cause-and-effect arrows are HYPOTHESES, not decoration

Every arrow on a strategy map represents a testable claim:
"improving [X] enables [Y]." K&N: "Strategy maps show the
cause-and-effect links by which specific improvements create desired
outcomes." Without the arrows, the map is a KPI list.

Arrows typically run:

- Capacity → Internal Process (skills enable better process execution)
- Internal Process → Customer (process improvements deliver customer
  outcomes)
- Customer → Financial (customer behaviour produces financial results)

Some arrows run within a perspective (e.g. "decrease operating costs"
contributes to "increase profitability" within the Financial
perspective).

## The "What's Missing?" diagnostic pattern

A strategy map's structural grid forces visibility. K&N's example:
Mobil discovered that one business unit had no objectives or measures
for dealers (when dealers were strategically critical), and another
had no measure for quality. The map exposed these gaps.

When generating a strategy map from public data, identify 2-4 gaps:

- Perspectives with under-developed objectives.
- Internal-process categories with no representation (e.g. no
  innovation objectives in a company claiming product leadership).
- Capacity objectives implied by the strategy but not visible in
  public materials.
- Customer-value-proposition components missing from the customer
  perspective (e.g. "speedy purchase" attribute missing for an
  operational-excellence company).

These gaps become deep-dive conversation starters.

## Anti-pattern: the KPI scorecard illusion

K&N: "Many organizations have built stakeholder scorecards, not
strategy scorecards, by developing a seemingly balanced measurement
system around three dominant groups of constituents: employees,
customers, and shareholders. A strategy, however, must describe HOW
a company will achieve its desired outcome." A list of metrics without
the arrows that connect them is "a dangerous illusion."

When generating, do not produce balanced lists of metrics across
perspectives without articulating the cause-and-effect relationships
that connect them. Arrows first; objectives second.

## The honesty caveat

K&N: "We do not claim to have made a science of strategy; the
formulation of great strategies is an art, and it will always remain
so. But the description of strategy should not be an art."

For our case: AI-generated strategy maps DESCRIBE strategy from
public signals. The FORMULATION of a real strategy — what the company
should actually do — remains a human conversation. Vector Advisory's
deep-dive engagement is where formulation happens. The generated map
is the input to that conversation, not the output of it.

## Time-phasing of strategy returns (useful framing)

When discussing the relative impact of strategy map elements:

- Operational improvements pay off in the SHORT term (months).
- Customer-relationship improvements pay off in the INTERMEDIATE term
  (1–3 years).
- Innovation pays off in the LONG term (3–5+ years).

A complete strategy generates returns from all three. A map weighted
exclusively toward operational excellence indicates a short-horizon
strategy; weighted toward innovation indicates a long-horizon
strategy. The mix is itself diagnostic.
